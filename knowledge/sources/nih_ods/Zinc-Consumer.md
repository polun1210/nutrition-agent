# Zinc

## What is zinc and what does it do?

[Zinc](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) is a [nutrient](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) that people need to stay healthy. Zinc is found in [cells](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) throughout the body. It helps your [immune system](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) fight off invading [bacteria](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) and [viruses](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#). Your body also uses zinc to make [DNA](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) (the [genetic](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) material in cells) and [proteins](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#). During pregnancy, infancy, childhood, and adolescence the body needs zinc to grow and develop properly. Zinc also helps wounds heal and is important for the proper sense of taste.

## How much zinc do I need?

The amount of zinc you need each day depends on your age. Average daily recommended amounts for different ages are listed below in [milligrams](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) (mg).

| Life Stage | Recommended Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Birth to 6 months | 2 mg |
| [Infants](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) 7–12 months | 3 mg |
| Children 1–3 years | 3 mg |
| Children 4–8 years | 5 mg |
| Children 9–13 years | 8 mg |
| Teen males 14–18 years | 11 mg |
| Teen females 14–18 years | 9 mg |
| Adult males | 11 mg |
| Adult females | 8 mg |
| Pregnant teens | 12 mg |
| Pregnant women | 11 mg |
| Breastfeeding teens | 13 mg |
| Breastfeeding women | 12 mg |

## What foods provide zinc?

Many foods contain zinc. You can get recommended amounts of zinc by eating a variety of foods including the following:

- Oysters, which have very high amounts of zinc.
- Meat, fish, [poultry](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), seafood such as crab and lobsters, and [fortified](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) breakfast cereals are also good sources of zinc.
- Beans, nuts, [whole grains](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), eggs, and [dairy](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) products provide some zinc.

## What kinds of zinc dietary supplements are available?

Almost all [multivitamin/mineral dietary supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) contain zinc. Zinc is also available alone or combined with [calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), [magnesium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), or other [ingredients](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) in [dietary supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#).

Dietary [supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) can have several different forms of zinc, such as zinc sulfate, zinc acetate, and zinc gluconate. It’s not clear whether one form is better than the others.

Zinc is also found in some denture adhesive creams and over-the-counter products, including those [labeled](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) as [homeopathic](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) medications for colds.

## Am I getting enough zinc?

Most people in the United States get enough zinc from the foods they eat.

However, certain groups of people may have trouble getting enough zinc:

- People who have had [gastrointestinal](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) surgery, such as weight loss surgery, or people who have [digestive disorders](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), such as [ulcerative colitis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) or [Crohn’s disease](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#). These conditions can decrease the amount of zinc that the body [absorbs](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) and increase the amount that is lost in [urine](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#).
- People who follow vegetarian or vegan diets because they do not eat meat, which is a good source of zinc. Also, beans and grains contain phytates that reduce the amount of zinc the body absorbs. [Vegetarians](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) and vegans might benefit from taking zinc supplements.
- Women who are pregnant or breastfeeding because they need more zinc for their growing baby and to make breast milk.
- Older infants who are breastfed because breast milk does not provide enough zinc for infants over 6 months of age. Older infants should be given foods that have zinc, such as pureed meats.
- Children who have [sickle cell disease](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), possibly because the medications they take can cause low levels of zinc. These children might benefit from taking zinc supplements.
- People who have alcohol use disorder, because alcohol reduces the amount of zinc the body absorbs and increases the amount that is lost in urine. Also, people with alcohol use disorder tend to consume lower amounts of nutrients, including zinc.

## What happens if I don’t get enough zinc?

Zinc [deficiency](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) causes [diarrhea](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), slow growth, and loss of appetite in infants and children. Infants and children who have had a zinc deficiency may have reproductive problems when they become adults. In older children, zinc deficiency also causes hair loss and frequent [infections](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#).

Zinc deficiency at any age can cause a loss of taste and smell. In older adults, zinc deficiency can delay wound healing and cause problems with thinking, reasoning, and memory.

In lower income countries, zinc deficiency during pregnancy can cause premature births and other complications. Babies may have low weight at birth and a higher [risk](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) of death.

Many of these [symptoms](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) can be [signs](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) of problems other than a zinc deficiency. If you have any of these symptoms, your health care provider can help determine if you might have a zinc deficiency.

## What are some effects of zinc on health?

Scientists are studying zinc to better understand how it affects health. Here are several examples of what research on zinc has shown.

### The common cold

Some studies suggest that zinc [lozenges](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) or zinc syrup speeds recovery from the [common cold](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) if you start taking them at the start of a cold. However, these products don’t seem to affect the severity of cold symptoms. More study is needed to determine the best [dose](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) and form of zinc for the common cold as well as how often and how long it should be taken.

### Pneumonia in children

Some studies in lower income countries show that zinc supplements lower the risk of [pneumonia](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) in young children. Zinc doesn’t seem to speed recovery or reduce the number of deaths from pneumonia.

### HIV in children and adults

Many people with HIV have low zinc levels. This occurs because they have trouble absorbing zinc from food. They also often have diarrhea, which increases zinc loss. Some studies show that supplemental zinc decreases diarrhea and complications of HIV, but other studies do not show this. Zinc supplements do not appear to reduce the risk of death in people with HIV. More research is needed to determine whether zinc supplements might help people with HIV.

### Childhood diarrhea

Children in developing countries often die from diarrhea. Studies show that zinc supplements help reduce the [duration](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) of diarrhea in these children, many of whom are zinc deficient or otherwise [malnourished](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#). The [World Health Organization](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) and [UNICEF](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) recommend that children with diarrhea take zinc for 10–14 days (20 mg/day, or 10 mg/day for infants under 6 months). It’s not clear whether zinc supplements help [treat](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) diarrhea in children who get enough zinc, such as most children in the United States.

### Age-related macular degeneration

Age-related macular degeneration ( [AMD](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#)) is an eye disease that gradually causes vision loss. In large studies among older people with AMD who were at high risk of developing advanced AMD, those who took daily dietary supplements with zinc and other ingredients for 5 years had a lower risk of developing advanced AMD than those who did not take the supplements. The ingredients in the supplements were: 80 mg zinc plus [vitamin E](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), [vitamin C](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), [copper](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), and either [beta-carotene](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) or lutein and zeaxanthin. People who have or are developing AMD should talk with their doctor about taking a dietary supplement called AREDS or [AREDS2](https://www.nei.nih.gov/learn-about-eye-health/eye-conditions-and-diseases/age-related-macular-degeneration/nutritional-supplements-age-related-macular-degeneration).

### Type 2 diabetes

People with type 2 diabetes often have low zinc levels. Some research shows that zinc supplements might help lower blood sugar and [cholesterol](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) levels. However, more research is needed to learn if zinc might be recommended for people with type 2 diabetes.

## Can zinc be harmful?

Yes, too much zinc can be harmful. Signs of too much zinc include [nausea](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), dizziness, headaches, upset stomach, vomiting, and loss of appetite. If you take too much zinc for a long time, you could have problems such as lower [immunity](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), low levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (good) cholesterol, and low copper levels. Taking very high doses of supplemental zinc can reduce your body’s absorption of magnesium.

Using large amounts of denture creams that contain zinc, well beyond what the label recommends, could lead to excessive zinc intake and copper deficiency. This can cause [neurological](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) problems, including loss of coordination, numbness, and weakness in the arms, legs, and feet.

The daily [upper limits](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) for zinc include intakes from all sources—foods, beverages, supplements, and medications. The chart below lists the amounts by age group. These upper limits do not apply to people who take supplemental zinc for medical reasons under the care of a doctor.

| Ages | Upper Limit |
| --- | --- |
| Birth to 6 months | 4 mg |
| Infants 7–12 months | 5 mg |
| Children 1–3 years | 7 mg |
| Children 4–8 years | 12 mg |
| Children 9–13 years | 23 mg |
| Teens 14–18 years | 34 mg |
| Adults | 40 mg |

## Does zinc interact with medications or other dietary supplements?

Yes. Zinc dietary supplements can [interact](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) or interfere with some medicines you might take. In some cases, medicines can lower zinc levels in your body. Here are several examples:

- Both quinolone [antibiotics](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) (such as Cipro) and tetracycline antibiotics (such as Achromycin and Sumycin) might reduce the amount of both zinc and the antibiotic that your body absorbs. To help avoid this interaction, take the antibiotic at least 2 hours before, or 4 to 6 hours after, taking a zinc supplement.
- Penicillamine is a [drug](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) used to treat [rheumatoid arthritis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) and Wilson disease. Zinc supplements can reduce the amount of penicillamine that your body absorbs. To help avoid this interaction, take zinc supplements and penicillamine at least 1 hour apart.
- [Thiazide diuretics](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), such as chlorthalidone (Hygroton) and hydrochlorothiazide (Esidrix and HydroDIURIL) increase the amount of zinc lost in urine. Taking thiazide [diuretics](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) for a long time might decrease the amount of zinc in your body.

Tell your doctor, [pharmacist](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), and other health care providers about any dietary supplements and [prescription](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#) or over-the-counter medicines you take. They can tell you if the dietary supplements might interact with your medicines. They can also explain whether the medicines you take might interfere with how your body absorbs or uses other nutrients.

## Zinc and healthful eating

People should get most of their nutrients from food and beverages, according to the federal government’s _[Dietary Guidelines for Americans](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#)_. Foods contain [vitamins](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), [minerals](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), [dietary fiber](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/#), and other components that benefit health. In some cases, fortified foods and dietary supplements are useful when it is not possible to meet needs for one or more nutrients (for example, during specific life stages such as pregnancy).
