# Vitamin E

Fact Sheet for Consumers

Expand All

_This is a general overview. For more in-depth information, see our_ [health professional fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-HealthProfessional/).

## What is vitamin E and what does it do?

[Vitamin E](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) is a [fat-soluble](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) [nutrient](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) found in many foods. In the body, it acts as an [antioxidant](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), helping to protect [cells](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) from the damage caused by [free radicals](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#). Free radicals are [compounds](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) formed when our bodies convert the food we eat into energy. People are also exposed to free radicals in the environment from cigarette smoke, air pollution, and [ultraviolet light](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) from the sun.

The body also needs [vitamin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) E to boost its [immune system](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) so that it can fight off invading [bacteria](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and [viruses](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#). It helps to widen [blood vessels](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and keep blood from clotting within them. In addition, cells use vitamin E to [interact](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) with each other and to carry out many important functions.

## How much vitamin E do I need?

The amount of vitamin E you need each day depends on your age. Average daily recommended amounts are listed below in [milligrams](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) (mg).

| Life Stage | Recommended Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Birth to 6 months | 4 mg |
| [Infants](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) 7–12 months | 5 mg |
| Children 1–3 years | 6 mg |
| Children 4–8 years | 7 mg |
| Children 9–13 years | 11 mg |
| Teens 14–18 years | 15 mg |
| Adults | 15 mg |
| Pregnant teens and women | 15 mg |
| Breastfeeding teens and women | 19 mg |

## What foods provide vitamin E?

Vitamin E is found naturally in foods and is added to some [fortified](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) foods. You can get recommended amounts of vitamin E by eating a variety of foods including the following:

- Vegetable oils like wheat germ, sunflower, and safflower oils are among the best sources of vitamin E. Corn and [soybean](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) oils also provide some vitamin E.
- Nuts (such as peanuts, hazelnuts, and, especially, almonds) and seeds (like sunflower seeds) are also among the best sources of vitamin E.
- Green vegetables, such as spinach and broccoli, provide some vitamin E.
- Food companies add vitamin E to some breakfast cereals, fruit juices, margarines and spreads, and other foods. To find out which ones have vitamin E, check the product [labels](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#).

## What kinds of vitamin E dietary supplements are available?

Vitamin E [supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) come in different amounts and forms. Two main things to consider when choosing a vitamin E supplement are:

1. The amount of vitamin E: Most once-daily multivitamin/mineral supplements provide about 13.5 mg of vitamin E, whereas vitamin E-only supplements commonly contain 67 mg or more. The [doses](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) in most vitamin E-only supplements are much higher than the recommended amounts. Some people take large doses because they believe or hope that doing so will keep them healthy or lower their [risk](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) of certain diseases.
2. The form of vitamin E: Although vitamin E sounds like a single substance, it is actually the name of eight related compounds in food, including [alpha-tocopherol](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#). Each form has a different potency, or level of activity in the body.

Vitamin E from natural sources is commonly listed as _d_-alpha-tocopherol on food packaging and supplement labels. [Synthetic](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) (laboratory-made) vitamin E is commonly listed as _dl_-alpha-tocopherol. The natural form is more potent; 1 mg vitamin E = 1 mg _d_-alpha-tocopherol (natural vitamin E) = 2 mg _dl_-alpha-tocopherol (synthetic vitamin E).

Some food and [dietary supplement](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) labels still list vitamin E in [International Units](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) ( [IUs](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#)) rather than mg. One IU of the natural form of vitamin E is equivalent to 0.67 mg. One IU of the synthetic form of vitamin E is equivalent to 0.45 mg.

Some vitamin E supplements provide other forms of the vitamin, such as gamma-tocopherol, tocotrienols, and mixed tocopherols. Scientists do not know if any of these forms are superior to alpha-tocopherol in supplements.

## Am I getting enough vitamin E?

The diets of most Americans provide less than the recommended amounts of vitamin E. Nevertheless, healthy people rarely show any clear [signs](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) that they are not getting enough vitamin E (see next question for information on the signs of vitamin E [deficiency](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#)).

## What happens if I don’t get enough vitamin E?

Vitamin E deficiency is very rare in healthy people. It is almost always linked to certain diseases in which fat is not properly digested or [absorbed](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#). Examples include [Crohn’s disease](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), [cystic fibrosis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), and certain rare [genetic](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) diseases such as [abetalipoproteinemia](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and [ataxia](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) with vitamin E deficiency (AVED). Vitamin E needs some fat for the digestive system to absorb it.

Vitamin E deficiency can cause [nerve](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and muscle damage that results in loss of feeling in the arms and legs, loss of body movement [control](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), muscle weakness, and vision problems. Another sign of deficiency is a weakened immune system.

## What are some effects of vitamin E on health?

Scientists are studying vitamin E to understand how it affects health. Here are several examples of what this research has shown.

### Heart disease

Some studies link higher intakes of vitamin E from supplements to lower chances of developing [coronary heart disease](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#). However, the best research finds no benefit. People in these studies are randomly assigned to take vitamin E or a [placebo](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) (dummy pill with no vitamin E or active [ingredients](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#)) and they don’t know which they are taking. Vitamin E supplements do not seem to [prevent](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) heart disease, reduce its severity, or affect the risk of death from this disease. Scientists do not know whether high intakes of vitamin E might protect the heart in younger, healthier people who do not have a high risk of heart disease.

### Cancer

Most research indicates that vitamin E does not help prevent [cancer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and may be harmful in some cases. Large doses of vitamin E have not consistently reduced the risk of [colon](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and [breast cancer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) in studies, for example. A large study found that taking vitamin E supplements (180 mg/day \[400 IU\]) for several years increased the risk of developing [prostate cancer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) in men. Two studies that followed middle-age men and women for 7 or more years found that extra vitamin E (201−268 mg/day \[300–400 IU\], on average) did not protect them from any form of cancer. However, one study found a link between the use of vitamin E supplements for 10 years or more and a lower risk of death from [bladder cancer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#).

Vitamin E dietary supplements and other antioxidants might interact with [chemotherapy](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and [radiation therapy](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#). People undergoing these treatments should talk with their doctor or oncologist before taking vitamin E or other antioxidant supplements, especially in high doses.

### Eye disorders

[Age-related macular degeneration](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) (AMD), or the loss of central vision in older people, and [cataracts](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) are among the most common causes of vision loss in older people. The results of research on whether vitamin E can help prevent these conditions are inconsistent. Among people with AMD who were at high risk of developing advanced AMD, a supplement containing large doses of vitamin E combined with other antioxidants, [zinc](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), and [copper](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) showed promise for slowing down the rate of vision loss.

### Mental function

Several studies have investigated whether vitamin E supplements might help older adults remain mentally alert and active as well as prevent or slow the decline of mental function and [Alzheimer’s disease](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#). So far, the research provides little [evidence](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) that taking vitamin E supplements can help healthy people or people with mild mental functioning problems to maintain brain health.

## Can vitamin E be harmful?

Vitamin E that is naturally present in food and beverages is not harmful and does not need to be limited.

In supplement form, however, high doses of vitamin E might increase the risk of bleeding (by reducing the blood’s ability to form clots after a cut or injury) and of serious bleeding in the brain (known as hemorrhagic [stroke](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#)). Because of this risk, the [upper limit](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) for adults is 1,000 mg/day for supplements of either natural or synthetic vitamin E. This is equal to 1,500 IU/day for natural vitamin E supplements and 1,100 IU/day for synthetic vitamin E supplements. The upper limits for children are lower than those for adults. Some research suggests that taking vitamin E supplements even below these upper limits might cause harm. In one study, for example, men who took 400 IU (180 mg) of synthetic vitamin E each day for several years had an increased risk of prostate cancer.

## Does vitamin E interact with medications or other dietary supplements?

Vitamin E dietary supplements can interact or interfere with certain medicines that you take. Here are some examples:

- Vitamin E can increase the risk of bleeding in people taking [anticoagulant](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) or antiplatelet medicines, such as warfarin (Coumadin).
- In one study, vitamin E plus other antioxidants (such as [vitamin C](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), [selenium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), and [beta-carotene](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#)) reduced the heart-protective effects of two [drugs](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) taken in combination (a [statin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and [niacin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#)) to affect blood [cholesterol](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) levels.
- Taking antioxidant supplements while undergoing chemotherapy or radiation therapy for cancer could alter the [effectiveness](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) of these treatments.

Tell your doctor, [pharmacist](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), and other health care providers about any dietary supplements and medicines you take. They can tell you if those dietary supplements might interact or interfere with your [prescription](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) or over-the-counter medicines, or if the medicines might interfere with how your body absorbs, uses, or breaks down nutrients.

## Vitamin E and healthful eating

People should get most of their nutrients from food and beverages, according to the federal government’s _[Dietary Guidelines for Americans](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#)._ Foods contain vitamins, [minerals](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#), [dietary fiber](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/#) and other components that benefit health. In some cases, fortified foods and dietary supplements are useful when it is not possible to meet needs for one or more nutrients (for example, during specific life stages such as pregnancy).
