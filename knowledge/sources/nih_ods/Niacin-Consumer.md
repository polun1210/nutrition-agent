# Niacin

Fact Sheet for Consumers

Expand All

_This is a general overview. For more in-depth information, see our_ [health professional fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-HealthProfessional/).

## What is niacin and what does it do?

[Niacin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) (also called [vitamin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) B3) helps turn the food you eat into the energy you need. Niacin is important for the development and function of the [cells](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) in your body.

## How much niacin do I need?

The amount of niacin you need depends on your age and sex. Average daily recommended amounts are listed below in [milligrams](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) (mg) of niacin equivalents (NE) (except for [infants](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) in their first 6 months).

The mg NE measure is used because your body can also make niacin from tryptophan, an [amino acid](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) in [proteins](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#). For example, when you eat turkey, which is high in tryptophan, some of this amino acid is converted to niacin in your [liver](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#). Using mg NE accounts for both the niacin you [consume](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) and the niacin your body makes from tryptophan. Infants in their first 6 months do not make much niacin from tryptophan.

| Life Stage | Recommended Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Birth to 6 months | 2 mg |
| Infants 7–12 months | 4 mg NE |
| Children 1–3 years | 6 mg NE |
| Children 4–8 years | 8 mg NE |
| Children 9–13 years | 12 mg NE |
| Teen boys 14–18 years | 16 mg NE |
| Teen girls 14–18 years | 14 mg NE |
| Adult men 19+ years | 16 mg NE |
| Adult women 19+ years | 14 mg NE |
| Pregnant teens and women | 18 mg NE |
| Breastfeeding teens and women | 17 mg NE |

## What foods provide niacin?

Niacin is found naturally in many foods and is added to some foods. You can get recommended amounts of niacin by eating a variety of foods, including the following:

- Animal foods, such as [poultry](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), beef, pork, and fish
- Some types of nuts, [legumes](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), and grains
- [Enriched](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) and [fortified](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) foods, such as many breads and cereals

## What kinds of niacin dietary supplements are available?

Niacin is found in multivitamin/mineral [supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#). It is also available in B-complex [dietary supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) and supplements containing only niacin. The two main forms of niacin in dietary supplements are nicotinic acid and nicotinamide.

Niacin (in the form of nicotinic acid) is also available as a [prescription](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) medicine used to [treat](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) high blood [cholesterol](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) levels.

## Am I getting enough niacin?

Most people in the United States get enough niacin from the foods they eat. Niacin [deficiency](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) is very rare in the United States. However, some people are more likely than others to have trouble getting enough niacin:

- Undernourished people with AIDS, alcohol use [disorder](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), [anorexia](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), [inflammatory bowel disease](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), or liver [cirrhosis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#)
- People whose diet has too little [iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), [riboflavin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), or [vitamin B6](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#); these [nutrients](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) are needed to convert tryptophan to niacin
- People with Hartnup disease, a rare [genetic disorder](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#)
- People with carcinoid syndrome, a condition in which slow-growing [tumors](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) develop in the [gastrointestinal tract](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#)

## What happens if I don’t get enough niacin?

You can develop niacin deficiency if you don’t get enough niacin or tryptophan from the foods you eat. Severe niacin deficiency leads to a disease called pellagra. Pellagra, which is uncommon in developed countries, can have the following effects:

- Rough skin that turns red or brown in the sun
- A bright red tongue
- Vomiting, [constipation](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), or [diarrhea](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#)
- [Depression](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#)
- Headaches
- Extreme tiredness
- Aggressive, paranoid, or suicidal behavior
- Hallucinations, [apathy](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), loss of memory

In its final stages, pellagra leads to loss of appetite followed by death.

## What are some effects of niacin on health?

Scientists are studying niacin to better understand how it affects health. Here is an example of what this research has shown.

### Cardiovascular disease

Scientists have studied the use of large [doses](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) of niacin in the form of nicotinic acid to help reduce the [risk](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) of [heart attack](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) and [stroke](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) in people with [atherosclerosis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#). They found that prescription-strength nicotinic acid (more than 100 times the [recommended dietary allowance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#)) can lower blood levels of LDL (bad) cholesterol, raise levels of HDL (good) cholesterol, and lower levels of [triglycerides](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#). However, these favorable effects on blood lipids (fats) don’t affect the risk of having a [cardiovascular event](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), such as heart attack, [sudden cardiac death](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), or stroke. In addition, experts do not recommend high doses of nicotinic acid for people taking a [statin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) medication.

Your health care provider should approve and supervise any use of very high doses of nicotinic acid (in the thousands of milligrams) to treat atherosclerosis.

## Can niacin be harmful?

The niacin that food and beverages naturally contain is safe. However, dietary supplements with 30 mg or more of nicotinic acid can make the skin on your face, arms, and chest turn red and burn, tingle, and itch. This can also lead to headaches, rashes, and dizziness.

If you take nicotinic acid as a medication in doses of 1,000 or more mg/day, it can cause more severe [side effects](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#). These include:

- Low blood pressure (which can increase the risk of falls)
- Extreme tiredness
- High [blood sugar](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) levels
- [Nausea](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), heartburn, and abdominal pain
- Blurred or impaired vision and fluid buildup in the eyes

Long-term treatment, especially with [extended-release](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) forms of nicotinic acid, can cause liver problems, including [hepatitis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) and liver failure.

Niacin in the form of nicotinamide has fewer side effects than nicotinic acid. However, at high doses of 500 mg/day or more, nicotinamide can cause diarrhea and easy bruising and can increase bleeding from wounds. Even higher doses of 3,000 mg/day or more can cause nausea, vomiting, and liver damage.

The daily [upper limits](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) for niacin from dietary supplements are listed below.

| Ages | Upper Limit |
| --- | --- |
| Birth to 12 months | Not established |
| Children 1–3 years | 10 mg |
| Children 4–8 years | 15 mg |
| Children 9–13 years | 20 mg |
| Teens 14–18 years | 30 mg |
| Adults 19+ years | 35 mg |

## Does niacin interact with medications or other dietary supplements?

Niacin dietary supplements can [interact](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) or interfere with certain medicines that you take, and some medicines can lower niacin levels in your body. Here are some examples:

- [Tuberculosis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) [drugs](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) (such as isoniazid and pyrazinamide) interfere with the body’s ability to convert tryptophan to niacin. This interference can lower niacin levels in your body.
- High doses of nicotinic acid (1,500 mg/day or more) can raise blood sugar levels and interfere with the [effectiveness](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) of [diabetes](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#) medications. These doses can even raise blood sugar levels in people who don’t have diabetes.

Tell your doctor, [pharmacist](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), and other health care providers about any dietary supplements and prescription or over-the-counter medicines you take. They can tell you if the dietary supplements might interact with your medicines. They can also tell you if the medicines might interfere with how your body [absorbs](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), uses, or breaks down niacin and other nutrients.

## Niacin and healthful eating

People should get most of their nutrients from food and beverages, according to the federal government’s _[Dietary Guidelines for Americans](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#)._ Foods contain vitamins, [minerals](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), [dietary fiber](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/#), and other components that benefit health. In some cases, fortified foods and dietary supplements are useful when it is not possible to meet needs for one or more nutrients (for example, during specific life stages such as pregnancy).
