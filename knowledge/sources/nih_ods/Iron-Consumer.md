# Iron

Fact Sheet for Consumers

Expand All

_This is a general overview. For more in-depth information, see our_ [health professional fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/).

## What is iron and what does it do?

[Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) is a [mineral](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) that the body needs for growth and development. Your body uses iron to make [hemoglobin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), a [protein](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) in [red blood cells](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) that carries oxygen from the [lungs](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) to all parts of the body, and myoglobin, a protein that provides oxygen to muscles. Your body also needs iron to make some [hormones](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#).

## How much iron do I need?

The amount of iron you need each day depends on your age, your sex, and whether you [consume](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) a mostly plant-based diet. Average daily recommended amounts are listed below in [milligrams](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) (mg). [Vegetarians](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) who do not eat meat, [poultry](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), or seafood need almost twice as much iron as listed in the table because the body doesn’t [absorb](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) nonheme iron in plant foods as well as heme iron in animal foods.

| Life Stage | Recommended Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Birth to 6 months | 0.27 mg |
| [Infants](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) 7–12 months | 11 mg |
| Children 1–3 years | 7 mg |
| Children 4–8 years | 10 mg |
| Children 9–13 years | 8 mg |
| Teen boys 14–18 years | 11 mg |
| Teen girls 14–18 years | 15 mg |
| Adult men 19–50 years | 8 mg |
| Adult women 19–50 years | 18 mg |
| Adults 51 years and older | 8 mg |
| Pregnant teens | 27 mg |
| Pregnant women | 27 mg |
| Breastfeeding teens | 10 mg |
| Breastfeeding women | 9 mg |

## What foods provide iron?

Iron is found naturally in many foods and is added to some [fortified](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) food products. You can get recommended amounts of iron by eating a variety of foods, including the following:

- Lean meat, seafood, and poultry
- Iron-fortified breakfast cereals and breads
- White beans, lentils, spinach, [kidney](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) beans, and peas
- Nuts and some dried fruits, such as raisins

Iron in food comes in two forms: heme iron and nonheme iron. Nonheme iron is found in plant foods and iron-fortified food products. Meat, seafood, and poultry have both heme and nonheme iron.

Your body absorbs iron from plant sources better when you eat it with meat, poultry, seafood, and foods that contain [vitamin C](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), such as citrus fruits, strawberries, sweet peppers, tomatoes, and broccoli.

## What kinds of iron dietary supplements are available?

Iron is available in many multivitamin/mineral [supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) and in supplements that contain only iron. Iron in supplements is often in the form of ferrous sulfate, ferrous gluconate, ferric citrate, or ferric sulfate. [Dietary supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) that contain iron have a statement on the [label](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) warning that they should be kept out of the reach of children. Accidental overdose of iron-containing products is a leading cause of [fatal](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) poisoning in children under 6.

## Am I getting enough iron?

Most people in the United States get enough iron. However, certain groups of people are more likely than others to have trouble getting enough iron:

- Teen girls and women with heavy periods
- Pregnant women and teens
- Infants (especially if they are premature or low-birth weight)
- Frequent blood donors
- People with [cancer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), [gastrointestinal](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) (GI) [disorders](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), or [heart failure](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#)

## What happens if I don't get enough iron?

In the short term, getting too little iron does not cause obvious [symptoms](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#). The body uses its stored iron in the muscles, [liver](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), [spleen](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), and [bone marrow](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#). However, when levels of iron stored in the body become low, iron [deficiency](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) [anemia](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) sets in. Red blood [cells](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) become smaller and contain less hemoglobin. As a result, blood carries less oxygen from the lungs throughout the body.

Symptoms of iron deficiency anemia include GI upset, weakness, tiredness, lack of energy, and problems with concentration and memory. In addition, people with iron deficiency anemia are less able to fight off germs and [infections](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), to work and exercise, and to [control](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) their body temperature. Infants and children with iron deficiency anemia might develop learning difficulties.

Iron deficiency is not uncommon in the United States, especially among young children, women under 50, and pregnant women. It can also occur in people who do not eat meat, poultry, or seafood; lose blood; have GI diseases that interfere with [nutrient](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) absorption; or eat poor diets.

## What are some effects of iron on health?

Scientists are studying iron to understand how it affects health. Iron’s most important contribution to health is [preventing](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) iron deficiency anemia and resulting problems.

### Pregnant women

During pregnancy, the amount of blood in a woman's body increases, so she needs more iron for herself and her growing baby. Getting too little iron during pregnancy increases a woman's [risk](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) of iron deficiency anemia and her infant's risk of low birth weight, premature birth, and low levels of iron. Getting too little iron might also harm her infant’s brain development.

Women who are pregnant should talk with their health care provider and take an iron supplement if recommended.

### Infants and toddlers

Iron deficiency anemia in infancy can lead to delayed psychological development, social withdrawal, and less ability to pay attention. By age 6 to 9 months, full-term infants could become iron deficient unless they eat iron- [enriched](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) solid foods or drink iron-fortified formula.

### Anemia of chronic disease

Some [chronic diseases](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#)—such as [rheumatoid arthritis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), [inflammatory bowel disease](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), and some types of cancer—can interfere with the body's ability to use its stored iron. Taking more iron from foods or supplements usually does not reduce the resulting anemia of [chronic](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) disease because iron is diverted from the blood circulation to storage sites. The main therapy for anemia of chronic disease is treatment of the underlying disease.

## Can iron be harmful?

Yes, iron can be harmful if you get too much. In healthy people, taking high [doses](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) of iron supplements (especially on an empty stomach) can cause an upset stomach, [constipation](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), [nausea](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), abdominal pain, vomiting, and [diarrhea](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#). Large amounts of iron might also cause more serious effects, including [inflammation](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) of the stomach lining and [ulcers](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#). High doses of iron can also decrease [zinc](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) absorption. Extremely high doses of iron (in the hundreds or thousands of mg) can cause organ failure, coma, convulsions, and death. Child-proof packaging and warning labels on iron supplements have greatly reduced the number of accidental iron poisonings in children.

Some people have an [inherited](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) condition called [hemochromatosis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) that causes [toxic](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) levels of iron to build up in their bodies. Without medical treatment, people with hereditary hemochromatosis can develop serious problems such as liver [cirrhosis](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), liver cancer, and heart disease. People with this disorder should avoid using iron supplements and [vitamin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) C supplements.

The daily [upper limits](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) for iron include intakes from all sources—food, beverages, and supplements—and are listed below. A doctor might [prescribe](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) more than the upper limit of iron to people who need higher doses for a while to [treat](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) iron deficiency.

| Ages | Upper Limit |
| --- | --- |
| Birth to 12 months | 40 mg |
| Children 1–13 years | 40 mg |
| Teens 14–18 years | 45 mg |
| Adults 19+ years | 45 mg |

## Does iron interact with medications or other dietary supplements?

Yes, iron supplements can [interact](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) or interfere with medicines and other supplements you take. Here are several examples:

- Iron supplements can reduce the amount of levodopa that the body absorbs, making it less effective. Levodopa, found in Sinemet and Stalevo, is used to treat [Parkinson’s disease](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) and [restless leg syndrome](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#).
- Taking iron with levothyroxine can reduce this medication’s [effectiveness](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#). Levothyroxine (Levothroid, Levoxyl, Synthroid, Tirosint, and Unithroid) is used to treat [hypothyroidism](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), [goiter](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), and [thyroid](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) cancer.
- The [proton pump inhibitors](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) lansoprazole (Prevacid) and omeprazole (Prilosec) decrease stomach acid, so they might reduce the amount of nonheme iron that the body absorbs from food.
- [Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#) might interfere with iron absorption. Taking calcium and iron supplements at different times of the day might prevent this problem.

Tell your doctor, [pharmacist](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), and other health care providers about any dietary supplements and prescription or over-the-counter medicines you take. They can tell you if the dietary supplements might interact with your medicines or if the medicines might interfere with how your body absorbs, uses, or breaks down nutrients.

## Iron and healthful eating

People should get most of their nutrients from food and beverages, according to the federal government’s _[Dietary Guidelines for Americans](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#)._ Foods contain vitamins, minerals, [dietary fiber](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/#), and other components that benefit health. In some cases, fortified foods and dietary supplements are useful when it is not possible to meet needs for one or more nutrients (for example, during specific life stages such as pregnancy).
